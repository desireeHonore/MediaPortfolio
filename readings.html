September 10, 2025

My use of photography throughout the years has differed drastically. Being born and raised in the 1990s, photography began as a sacred pastime. Something that was recognized for it's contributions to the world, bestowing the most precious treasures in history: memories…just as they were remembered years before. Fast forward to present day, the role photography plays in my life is significantly different, unreliable, untrustworthy to say the least. To be completely transparent, my use of photography and image making has been irresponsible, in the sense of totally abandoning the ingenuity and intentionality of photography and image making as an art and I hope that will change during the duration of this course.

“Design is not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works.” – Steve Jobs

Susan Sontag’s, “In Plato’s Cave”, provided a timeless journey throughout space and time. I could correspond with the text considering the dramatic transformation of photographic consumption today. Specifically, through channels such as social media, mass digital photo storage, and the justified glorification of photographers and their talent, as well as the physical photograph, something that was widely accessible to all, now becoming increasingly uncommon.

Through a retrospective lens, I can’t help but feel nostalgically connected to the significance and role the camera, and the photographs it took, especially within in my own household. It was a vessel of memory, shaping how experiences were recorded and retold, passing down from generation to generation, an heirloom. There wasn’t the opportunity to capture “the perfect shot”, but all the photos from the wind-up camera that wouldn’t be revealed until a week or two later were executed as the perfect shot, worthy of being placed in the accredited photo album (albeit some photos would prove visually indiscernible and wouldn’t make the cut). Photo albums and the pictures it contained was a necessity and how we stayed connected to the world around us, to our ancestors, our distant relatives and the treasured moments we made. The culture of photography, theoretically, resonated differently than the memories I create in the present.

I couldn’t imagine a world without the photographic rituals of the past, a world where I wouldn’t have captured my beloved late aunt when she was most full of like, but only photos held in my smartphone of her sick and confined to her hospital bed. It presents somewhat of a melancholy reflection. I am also reminded of the inherently digital black hole we find ourselves in, where without a backup of our precious timepieces, like your daughter’s first steps, they can be lost forever (yeah, I learned the hard way). The physicality of photographs may seem less significant these days, but I appreciate Sontag highlighting the profound impact it’s had on both past and present alike.

These considerations inspire me to represent visually photography, illustrations and text notions in a way that captures a feeling of responsibility. Responsibility to the subject, the camera and its boundless properties and ensuring that I express the message with the utmost sincerity, so the viewer can truly interpret its meaning. Incorporating that nostalgic feel to photography and illustration can evoke a sense of longing, serenity and culture.


October 10, 2025

Reflecting on the article “With ‘AI slop’ distorting our reality, the world is sleepwalking into disaster” by Nesrine Malik, my thoughts on artificial intelligence, specifically the pessimistic ones, were reinvigorated. Looking at the dramatic influx of ‘brain-rotting’ material that has seeped into what we call the new-age of technology, it almost feels as though what could have been an era of new innovations, creativities, ideas, and techniques, are being abused (even used), by those who don’t want to see generations continue to grow into something that can plant the seeds for future generations and so forth.

“We are amusing ourselves to death.” – Neil Postman
The growth of this generation’s AI resembles that of weeds...purposeless and needing to be removed from the field of tech so that what’s beneficial can survive. When Malik referred to the widespread platforms that these AI integrations became a part of, these platforms typically supported authentic content. For example, WhatsApp, a portal often held for genuine communication and sharing authentic photos/videos to immigrant family members across the world, sharing real-life stories of actual their actual day to day life. To the world’s dismay, the trajectory of AI in a world where authenticity never had to battle with anything has taken full control.

Currently, the fraudulent appearance of ‘ai slop’ is, in actuality, a good thing. Its unlikelihood allows us (might I add, our highly impressionable minds), as viewers, to differentiate between what’s real and what’s fake, and at this stage of nuance is the perfect time for regulation. Sadly, it appears that the ones who control regulation and disbursement of said channels are the ones who look to profit and maintain power over these tools, while marginalizing those who don’t…and those who speak out. I’m afraid creativity in the future will boil down to who can tell an AI bot, most fluently, what to create, or rather what to generate. Because humans create, it is an inherent ability that was granted to us, as an avid Bible-reader would say, by God because we’re made in his likeness.

While focusing on the human nature of creativity and design, when it comes to AI, without the proper labels and warnings, anyone can be everyone and no one at the same time. It leaves so many artists in a frenzy. I remember doom-scrolling, stripping away at my own creativity one short clip at a time, and I stumbled upon a creator who showed great concern about the replacement of AI when it came to his personal art. He created his own food art, videography of him cooking with incredible production and intimacy. The question that he began the video with was, “Do you think AI can replicate my content?”, and almost immediately, I replied yes. His art was perfect. So perfect, it appeared unreal. Two years ago, I would be in awe at his work and convinced it was content born from human hands, but now…not so much. And that was the epiphany, that I wouldn’t know the difference. While his work felt so much like AI, the truth felt stranger. Soon, the digital world will be oversaturated with AI generated media that we will long for the authentic material. It will be nostalgia, maybe even monetized in the powers of those who hold them. In the meantime, I will ensure I hold on to pieces of this world and the world I grew up in to pass along to future generations.


October 31, 2025

When I first read the article "Motherhood in Crisis" by Al Jazeera, I found myself deeply moved by the real stories it shared about mothers facing unimaginable challenges during pregnancy and childbirth. What struck me most was Memunatu's story, a woman living in Sierra Leone whose experience opened my eyes to the brutal reality of accessing medical care when hospitals seem impossibly far away and resources barely exist. As I moved through the article, taking in the illustrations, photographs, text, and videos, I realized how profoundly the illustrations shaped my understanding of her journey and allowed me to feel what she endured.

“What happened was the smartphone..." -James Marriot
The illustrations drew me in immediately with their simplicity and their comic-like, graphic novel aesthetic. Watching Memunatu walk those endless distances through the drawings, I could feel the exhaustion settling into my own bones. When the illustrations showed her waiting desperately for help, or when her face revealed fear and uncertainty, something shifted inside me. I began to understand that these drawings weren't trying to replicate reality the way photographs do, instead they were reaching for something deeper. While photographs captured the tangible world around her, showing real people and actual places, the illustrations seemed to bypass the surface entirely. They stripped away unnecessary details and zeroed in on what truly mattered: the emotional weight of each moment, the internal experience that no camera could ever capture.

I found myself thinking about what illustration could do that photography simply couldn't. The drawings let me glimpse Memunatu's inner world. Her worry, her doubt about whether help would arrive in time and the quiet terror that must have accompanied every step of her journey. A photograph would have shown me her face, but it couldn't reveal the thoughts racing through her mind or the fear in her heart. The illustrations also filled in gaps where cameras hadn't been present, reconstructing parts of her journey and past experiences that would otherwise remain invisible. This completeness made her story feel whole in a way that photographs alone never could.

What also resonated with me was how much easier the illustrations made it to truly grasp her reality. If I had encountered only text, I would have been left constructing everything in my imagination. Having never visited somewhere like Sierra Leone, the visual guides are crucial. The drawings showed me the rough roads, villages, and clinics, as if they built a world I could enter and understand. They transformed the article from something abstract into something tangible and immediate.

I also appreciated the dignity that illustration afforded Memunatu and the other mothers like her. I know firsthand, that childbirth involves intense physical and emotional pain, moments that are sacred and private. The thought of intrusive photographs documenting those vulnerable experiences would be appropriate to me. Illustrations offered a gentler alternative and convey the truth of what happened while maintaining respect and protecting the humanity of the people involved.

Reflecting on everything, I've come to see how essential illustration was to telling Memunatu's story with the power and sensitivity it deserved. The drawings worked alongside the photographs and videos, each medium contributing something unique, but it was the illustrations that truly let me feel her journey from the inside out.


December 12, 2025

When I first opened The New York Times interactive article "Nuclear War Prevention," I wasn't prepared for how deeply it would affect me. I expected statistics and political analysis, maybe some historical context. What I found instead was something that felt uncomfortably close to home, reminding me that nuclear war isn't just a chapter in the history books but a reality that exists right now, in our present moment.

“The only way to learn to code is to write a lot of code.” –Aristoteles
The article talked about how in the fall of 2022, U.S. intelligence believed that there was almost a 50/50 chance that Russia might use a tactical nuclear weapon in Ukraine. When I read that, my heart sank. 50/50 odds felt uncertain and impossibly high for something so catastrophic. This topic hits particularly close to home for me because I know a few people who live in Russia and Ukraine, and I’ve personally seen how they're living through this reality every single day. While I'm here reading about it from a distance, they're experiencing the weight of these tensions firsthand

What really got to me was realizing how much I'd been relying on assumptions that clearly don't hold up anymore. I always thought that the threat of complete annihilation would be enough to keep countries from ever using nuclear weapons. But the article made me question whether that logic still applies. In moments of desperation, miscommunication, or escalating conflict; rational thinking can disappear. Leaders might make decisions based on fear or pressure rather than careful calculation or even mercy.

The article also walked through what the actually reality of a nuclear weapon would be. It wasn't just about the immediate explosions and the people killed instantly. It explained how the smoke and soot from nuclear detonations would rise into the atmosphere, blocking out sunlight and causing temperatures to drop worldwide. Crops/agriculture would fail, food systems would collapse, meaning people who live thousands of miles away from any conflict zone could face starvation. I tried to imagine what that would look like, and it felt overwhelming. The idea that a weapon used in one part of the world could create a global famine made the danger feel inescapable and at a grand scale.

I've realized that I usually avoid thinking about nuclear war because it feels too big and too frightening to process. It's easier to push it to the back of my mind and focus on daily life. But this article didn't let me do that. It forced me to sit with the discomfort and acknowledge that the threat is real and growing. The agreements that once kept nuclear arsenals in check are falling apart, and tensions between nuclear-armed countries are rising.

What stayed with me most after reading was the sense that this isn't just something for world leaders to worry about. We all have a responsibility to stay informed and to understand what's at stake. It's uncomfortable and scary, but ignoring it won't make it go away.
